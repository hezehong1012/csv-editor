Why edit CSV in the browser instead of Excel?

Excel silently changes your data

Opening a CSV in Excel can strip leading zeros from ZIP codes, turn long account numbers into 1.23E+15, and rewrite dates in your local format. A plain-text CSV editor shows the file exactly as stored, so nothing is reformatted behind your back.

No install, works on locked-down machines

Nothing to download or license. Open the page in any modern browser — including corporate laptops where Office add-ins are blocked.

Confidential files stay on your machine

Unlike most "upload your CSV" services, the file is parsed locally with JavaScript. It never travels over the network, so customer lists and payroll data remain on your device.

Fixes files Excel refuses to open

Badly quoted fields, mixed line endings, stray delimiters and files that are too wide for Excel's sheet limit are all readable here.

Semicolon CSV, decimal commas and encodings

If a CSV opens with all the values crammed into one column, the file almost certainly uses a different delimiter than the comma. Excel in German, French, Italian, Dutch and most other European locales writes semicolon-separated files, because the comma is reserved as the decimal mark. The same happens with tab- and pipe-delimited exports from databases and CRMs.

This editor inspects the first lines of the file and picks the delimiter for you (comma, semicolon, tab or pipe); you can also force it from the import panel. The same applies to encoding: UTF-8, UTF-8 with BOM, Windows-1252 (typical for older European Excel files), and Shift-JIS / EUC-JP (Japanese Excel) are detected automatically, which removes the usual Ã¤, Ã¶, Ã© or question-mark garbage. When exporting you can choose comma or semicolon and UTF-8, BOM or legacy encodings, so the file opens correctly on the recipient's machine.

Does it work with large CSV files?
It runs within your browser's memory. Pagination keeps editing smooth for tens of thousands of rows.
Why does my CSV open with everything in one column?
That usually means the file uses a different delimiter than your spreadsheet expects — for example a semicolon-delimited file (common in European Excel) opened with comma settings. Can I open a European semicolon-separated CSV?
Yes. Files exported by Excel in German, French, Italian and other European locales often use a semicolon as the field separator because the comma is the decimal mark. The editor detects this automatically, and you can export back to either comma or semicolon when you are done.
